\chapter{Embedded System}
\label{ch:embedded}

This chapter describes the hardware and low-level software of the Cyclops rover. It covers
the rover's components and architecture, its closed-loop wheel speed control, the UART link
between its two boards, and the calibration of its single camera.

\section{System Overview}
The Cyclops rover is built from five main hardware components:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{TI-RSLK rover chassis}~\cite{ti_rslk_max} -- the mobile base, including the motors,
    wheel encoders, and motor driver.
  \item \textbf{MSP432 LaunchPad}~\cite{msp432_p4} -- the microcontroller that runs the low-level
    software.
  \item \textbf{Raspberry Pi 5}~\cite{rpi5_brief} -- the onboard computer that captures camera frames
    and runs the navigation loop.
  \item \textbf{MacBook Pro (M1)} -- an off-board laptop that runs monocular depth
    estimation on the frames streamed from the Raspberry Pi 5.
  \item \textbf{MKTechnic USB camera}~\cite{mktechnic_usb_camera} -- the single
    front-mounted camera, which has a $95^\circ$ field of view and is operated at
    a resolution of $640 \times 480$ pixels.
\end{itemize}

The rover runs entirely on battery power. The Raspberry Pi 5 is powered by a
Geekworm X1202 UPS HAT~\cite{geekworm_x1202}, which holds four 18650 lithium-ion
cells and lets the Raspberry Pi 5 run without a wall power supply. The TI-RSLK
chassis has its own power supply: a battery holder with six 1.2\,V cells.

Throughout this thesis, the onboard computer is always written out as
``Raspberry Pi 5'' or ``Raspberry Pi'' and never shortened to ``Pi.'' This
avoids confusion with the proportional--integral (PI) controller that the MSP432
uses to regulate motor speed.

on the chassis below it, and the USB camera is fixed to the front of the rover,
facing forward and titled down so that the near field in front of
the rover is in view.

  \label{fig:data-flow}

The computation is split across three devices:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Raspberry Pi} (high-level tasks):
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Captures camera frames
    \item Communicates with the laptop
    \item Runs the navigation loop (Section~\ref{sec:navigation})
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{MSP432 microcontroller} (low-level tasks):
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Counts encoder pulses (Section~\ref{sec:encoder})
    \item Runs the proportional--integral (PI) speed controller (Section~\ref{sec:PI})
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Laptop}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Runs the monocular depth estimation (Chapter~\ref{ch:mde})
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Each of these components is explained in more detail in later chapters; here, a brief
overview of the role each one plays is given. Monocular depth estimation gives the rover
the ability to perceive its environment by recovering the distance to obstacles from a
single camera image (Chapter~\ref{ch:mde}). The navigation loop uses these distances to
localize the rover, estimating where it is within its environment and issue movement commands
(Chapter~\ref{ch:localization}). The encoder counter tracks how far each wheel has turned,
which tells the rover how much it has moved (Section~\ref{sec:encoder}). Finally, the PI
speed controller regulates the speed of each motor so that the wheels turn at the speeds
requested by the navigation loop (Section~\ref{sec:PI}).

The rover is split into two boards because motor control needs precise, repeatable
timing. The PI controller must update at a fixed period, and every encoder pulse must be
counted for the measured speed to be correct. The Raspberry Pi OS~\cite{rpi_os} is a
general-purpose operating system that does not guarantee when a process is
scheduled~\cite{reghenzani2019rtlinux}, so camera capture, network traffic, and other
processes can delay the controller or cause pulses to be missed. The MSP432 instead runs
bare-metal code: hardware timers trigger the control update at a fixed period, hardware
PWM drives the motors without CPU involvement, and interrupts respond to each encoder
edge with minimal latency~\cite{msp432_p4}.

The Raspberry Pi provides what the microcontroller lacks: a camera interface, Wi-Fi for
the SSH tunnel to the laptop, and enough memory and processing power to run navigation
and localization in a high-level language. This split isolates the timing-critical loop
from the Raspberry Pi's variable workload. The boards exchange only compact UART
messages: target wheel speeds to the MSP432, and left and right encoder counts back to
the Raspberry Pi.

Loop rates are reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} for reference, but system latency and power
consumption are outside the scope of this thesis.

\section{PWM}
\label{sec:pwm}

The MSP432 sets how hard each motor is driven using pulse-width modulation (PWM). A PWM
signal is a square wave with a fixed period that switches between high and low. The
fraction of each period that the signal spends high is called the duty cycle~\cite{valvano}.
Figure~\ref{fig:pwm-duty} compares a 10\% duty cycle, where the signal is high for only
a short part of each period, with a 90\% duty cycle, where it is high for most of the
period.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/pwm-duty}
  \caption[PWM signals at 10\% and 90\% duty cycle]{PWM signals at 10\% and 90\% duty
    cycle. Both have the same period $T_{\mathrm{PWM}}$; only the fraction of each
    period spent high changes.}
  \label{fig:pwm-duty}
\end{figure}

The MSP432 generates two PWM signals, one for each motor. Both
signals come from the hardware timer Timer\_A0. Once the timer is configured, it
produces the signals on its own with no CPU involvement. To change a duty cycle, the
firmware only writes a new number to the timer.

The timer configuration follows Valvano~\cite{valvano}. 
The timer is clocked at 1.5\,MHz, so each tick lasts about 0.667\,\textmu s. It runs in
up-down mode: it counts from 0 up to a top value $P = 7{,}500$, then back down to 0,
and repeats.
Equation~\ref{eq:pwm-period} gives the resulting PWM period
$T_{\mathrm{PWM}}$, where $f_{\mathrm{clk}}$ is the timer clock frequency.
\begin{equation}
  T_{\mathrm{PWM}} = \frac{2P}{f_{\mathrm{clk}}}
  = \frac{2 \times 7{,}500}{1.5\,\text{MHz}} = 10\,\text{ms}
  \label{eq:pwm-period}
\end{equation}
This gives a PWM frequency of 100\,Hz.

Each motor's duty cycle is controlled by a single compare value $D$, an integer between
0 and $P$. The output goes high when the counter passes $D$ on its way down and goes low
when the counter passes $D$ on its way up. The signal is therefore high whenever the
counter is below $D$. Equation~\ref{eq:pwm-duty} gives the resulting duty cycle.
\begin{equation}
  \text{Duty cycle} = \frac{D}{P} = \frac{D}{7{,}500}
  \label{eq:pwm-duty}
\end{equation}
A value of $D = 0$ keeps the output low, so the motor is off. A value of
$D = 3{,}750$ gives a 50\% duty cycle, and values close to 7{,}500 give nearly full
power. The firmware rejects any value with $D \geq P$, so the largest usable value is
7{,}499.

Each PWM signal drives an H-bridge on the TI-RSLK chassis~\cite{valvano}, which switches the
battery voltage across the motor and sets its direction. A larger duty cycle applies
the battery voltage for a larger fraction of each period, so the motor spins faster.

\section{Control loop}
\label{sec:control-loop}

An open-loop controller sets a duty cycle and assumes it produces the desired wheel
speed. In practice, motors do not follow consistent speed with the same duty cycle
because the speed varies with
battery voltage, load, and differences between the two motors. Thus, our system adopts 
a closed-loop controller
which measures each wheel speed in real-time to correct their duty cycle command~\cite{valvano}.

The controller runs in discrete time: the MSP432 measures the wheel and updates the motor
command once per sample period. A quantity written with $[k]$ is
therefore a value taken at sample $k$, and a quantity without $[k]$ stays constant
between samples. The symbols used to describe the speed loop are defined as follows.
\begin{description}
  \item[$k$] The sample index, counting the timer interrupts since the loop started.
    Sample $k$ occurs at time $t = k\,T_s$.
  \item[$T_s$] The sample period, the fixed time between two samples
    ($T_s = 10$\,ms).
  \item[$\dot{C}^*$] The target wheel speed sent by the Raspberry Pi, in counts per second.
  \item[$\dot{C}^*\,T_s$] The same target speed converted to counts per sample. The encoder
    measures speed in counts per sample period, so the target must be expressed in the
    same units before the two can be compared. Multiplying by $T_s$ performs this
    conversion; for example, a target of $\dot{C}^* = 500$ counts per second becomes
    $\dot{C}^*\,T_s = 500 \times 0.01 = 5$ counts per sample.
  \item[{$\Delta C[k]$}] The measured wheel speed at sample $k$, in counts per sample,
    obtained from the number of encoder counts accumulated during the last sample period
    (Section~\ref{sec:encoder}).
  \item[{$e[k]$}] The speed error at sample $k$, the difference between the target
    $\dot{C}^*\,T_s$ and the measured speed $\Delta C[k]$.
  \item[{$u[k]$}] The controller output at sample $k$, a signed PWM duty value that sets
    the motor's drive strength and direction (Section~\ref{sec:PI}).
\end{description}

The target speed is sent in counts per second rather than RPM because it is transmitted as
an integer (Section~\ref{sec:uart-speed}), and one RPM equals $N/60 = 6$ counts per second,
so counts per second gives six times finer speed resolution. It is also the encoder's native
unit, so the MSP432 can use the target directly without converting units.

Figure~\ref{fig:control-loop} shows the closed speed loop that runs on the MSP432 for
each wheel. The target speed $\dot{C}^*\,T_s$ from the Raspberry Pi is compared against the
measured speed $\Delta C[k]$ to form the speed error $e[k]$. The PI controller converts
this error into a new duty cycle $u[k]$, which the PWM and H-bridge apply to the motor.
The encoder produces pulses as the wheel turns, and the speed calculation counts those
pulses over one sample period to obtain the next $\Delta C[k]$. The loop repeats every
sample period ($10$\,ms), so any difference between the target and actual speed is corrected
continuously. The encoder and speed calculation are described in
Section~\ref{sec:encoder}, and the PI controller in Section~\ref{sec:PI}.

  \centering

  \caption[Closed-loop wheel speed control]{Closed-loop wheel speed control. The
    measured speed $\Delta C[k]$ is fed back and subtracted from the target $\dot{C}^*\,T_s$;
    the PI controller acts on the resulting error $e[k]$.}
  \label{fig:control-loop}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}



The incremental rotary encoder, or simply encoder, on the TI-RSLK rover is a sensor 
that converts the rotation of the motor shaft into a train of electrical
pulses, with a fixed number of pulses per
revolution~\cite{valvano}. Counting the pulses tells how far the shaft has turned, and counting them
over a known time interval tells how fast it is turning. Each wheel of the Cyclops rover
has its own encoder. The MSP432 uses the counts to measure wheel speed for the PI
controller (Section~\ref{sec:PI}) and to report how far each wheel has traveled to the
Raspberry Pi.

The encoders on the Cyclops rover are optical. A slotted disk turns with the wheel, and
light passing through the slots falls on a photoresistor~\red{[valvano] I believe it is 
a photoresistor but have to double check with valvano book}. As the wheel turns, the slots
alternately pass and block the light, so the photoresistor output switches between high
and low once per slot. The disk has $N = 360$ slots per revolution. Thus, 360 pulses would be observed for one
full rotation of a wheel.

The Cyclops rover uses quadrature encoders, which detect the direction of rotation as well
as its speed. The MSP432 keeps a signed count $C$ for each wheel, which is incremented
when the wheel moves the rover forward and decremented when it moves the rover backward.
Readers interested in how quadrature encoding works are referred to~\cite{valvano}.


Wheel speed is sampled by a periodic timer interrupt on the MSP432 that fires every
$T_s = 10$\,ms. At each sampling, the interrupt service routine reads the current count of each wheel and
subtracts the count it read on the previous sample. Equation~\ref{eq:encoder-speed} gives
the wheel speed $\Delta C[k]$ at sample $k$, in counts per sample period.
\begin{equation}
  \Delta C[k] = C[k] - C[k-1]
  \label{eq:encoder-speed}
\end{equation}
The left and right speeds are passed directly to the PI controller
(Section~\ref{sec:PI}), so the controller works in counts per 10\,ms rather than in
physical units.

\subsection{PI Control}
\label{sec:PI}


The controller in the speed loop is a
PI controller~\red{[]}. Each wheel has its own controller, and
both run inside the 10\,ms timer interrupt described in Section~\ref{sec:encoder},
immediately after the wheel speeds $\Delta C[k]$ are measured.

Equation~\ref{eq:pi-error} gives the speed error $e[k]$ at sample $k$.
\begin{equation}
  e[k] = \dot{C}^*\,T_s - \Delta C[k]
  \label{eq:pi-error}
\end{equation}
The integral term accumulates this error over time. Equation~\ref{eq:pi-integral} gives
the integral term $I[k]$, where $K_I$ is the integral gain.
\begin{equation}
  I[k] = I[k-1] + K_I\,e[k]
  \label{eq:pi-integral}
\end{equation}
The controller output is the sum of a term proportional to the current error and the
integral term. Equation~\ref{eq:pi-output} gives the output $u[k]$, where $K_P$ is the
proportional gain.
\begin{equation}
  u[k] = K_P\,e[k] + I[k]
  \label{eq:pi-output}
\end{equation}
Both gains $K_P$ and $K_I$ have units of PWM duty per count per sample.


The sign of $u[k]$ sets the direction the wheel turns, and its magnitude sets the PWM
duty. The largest usable duty value is 7{,}499 (Section~\ref{sec:pwm}), so the output is
clamped to the range $[-7{,}499,\ 7{,}499]$.
Both wheels use the same gains, $K_P = 200$ and $K_I = 20$ which we chose after a 
testing a few values

No derivative term is used, since PI control is commonly recommended for motor speed
control~\cite{valvano} and preliminary tests showed it performs well enough for the rover.

The performance of the controller is evaluated with a step-response test. Starting from
no movement, the target speed of a wheel is changed abruptly from zero to a constant value, and
the measured speed $\Delta C[k]$ is recorded on every sample as the wheel speeds up. The
results of this test are presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. The step response is
judged by four measures~\cite{valvano}:\red{have to double check if these are specifically what
valvano says to measure}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rise time} -- the time the measured speed takes to go from 10\% to 90\%
    of the target. It shows how quickly the wheel responds to a new target.
  \item \textbf{Overshoot} -- how far the measured speed goes past the target before
    settling. Equation~\ref{eq:overshoot} gives the percent overshoot $M_p$, where
    $\Delta C_{\max}$ is the largest measured speed during the response and $\dot{C}^*\,T_s$ is
    the target in counts per sample.
    \begin{equation}
      M_p = \frac{\Delta C_{\max} - \dot{C}^*\,T_s}{\dot{C}^*\,T_s} \times 100\%
      \label{eq:overshoot}
    \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Settling time} -- the time after which the measured speed stays within
    $\pm 2\%$ of the target. It shows how quickly the wheel reaches a steady speed.
  \item \textbf{Steady-state error} -- the difference between the target and the measured
    speed after the response has settled. With an integral term, this error should go
    to about zero. Any error that persists keeps adding to $I[k]$
    (Equation~\ref{eq:pi-integral}), which keeps changing the output until the error is
    removed.
\end{itemize}

\section{UART}
\label{sec:uart}

The MSP432 and the Raspberry Pi communicate over a UART link at a baud rate of 115200
(Figure~\ref{fig:data-flow}).
Slot counts travel from the MSP432 to the Raspberry Pi, and target wheel speeds travel
in the opposite direction.

\subsection{Slot Count (MSP432 to Raspberry Pi)}
\label{sec:uart-slot-count}

Every 100\,ms, a timer interrupt on the MSP432 sends the current left and right slot
counts (Section~\ref{sec:encoder}) to the Raspberry Pi. The firmware briefly disables
interrupts while it copies the two counts, so both values come from the same instant.
Each count is a signed 32-bit integer and is sent in big-endian order, with the most
significant byte first.
Table~\ref{tab:slot-count-packet} shows the resulting 10-byte packet.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Slot count packet sent from the MSP432 to the Raspberry Pi.}
  \label{tab:slot-count-packet}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \hline
    Byte & Field & Description \\
    \hline
    0     & Start    & Constant \texttt{0xAA} \\
    1--4  & $C_L$    & Left slot count, bits 31:0 \\
    5--8  & $C_R$    & Right slot count, bits 31:0 \\
    9     & Checksum & XOR of bytes 1--8 \\
    \hline
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The start byte marks the beginning of a packet so that the Raspberry Pi can find
the packet boundaries in the byte stream. Equation~\ref{eq:uart-checksum} defines the
checksum $X$, where $b_1, \dots, b_8$ are the eight data bytes and $\oplus$ denotes
bitwise XOR.
\begin{equation}
  X = b_1 \oplus b_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus b_8
  \label{eq:uart-checksum}
\end{equation}
The Raspberry Pi recomputes $X$ from the received data bytes and discards the packet if
the result does not match the received checksum.

\subsection{Speed (Raspberry Pi to MSP432)}
\label{sec:uart-speed}

The Raspberry Pi sends the target left and right wheel speeds to the MSP432, where they
become the setpoints $\dot{C}^*_L$ and $\dot{C}^*_R$ of the PI controller (Section~\ref{sec:PI}). Unlike the slot count
packet, the speed command is not sent on a fixed schedule. The Raspberry Pi sends a new
command only when the navigation loop finishes an iteration, so the time between commands
depends on how fast that loop runs and the navigation process (Section~\ref{sec:navigation})

Each target speed is given in counts per second (Section~\ref{sec:control-loop}) and sent as a signed 16-bit integer in
big-endian order. The sign gives the direction of rotation, with positive values driving
the wheel forward. 
Table~\ref{tab:speed-packet} shows the resulting 6-byte packet.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Speed command packet sent from the Raspberry Pi to the MSP432.}
  \label{tab:speed-packet}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \hline
    Byte & Field & Description \\
    \hline
    0     & Start    & Constant \texttt{0xAA} \\
    1--2  & $\dot{C}^*_L$ & Left target speed, bits 15:0 \\
    3--4  & $\dot{C}^*_R$ & Right target speed, bits 15:0 \\
    5     & Checksum & XOR of bytes 1--4 \\
    \hline
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The packet uses the same framing as the slot count packet: a \texttt{0xAA} start byte
followed by an XOR checksum. Equation~\ref{eq:uart-speed-checksum} applies the checksum of
Equation~\ref{eq:uart-checksum} to the four data bytes $b_1, \dots, b_4$ of the speed
packet.
\begin{equation}
  X = b_1 \oplus b_2 \oplus b_3 \oplus b_4
  \label{eq:uart-speed-checksum}
\end{equation}
The MSP432 recomputes $X$ from the received data bytes and discards the packet if the
result does not match the received checksum. When a packet is discarded, both setpoints are
set to zero, stopping the rover until the next valid command arrives.

\section{Camera}
\label{sec:camera}

The MKTechnic USB camera uses a wide-angle fisheye lens. The wide lens lets the rover see
more of the scene in front of it, but it also bends straight lines in the image, most
visibly near the edges of the frame. The monocular depth estimation models used in this
thesis (Chapter~\ref{ch:mde}) are trained mostly on images taken with ordinary pinhole
 cameras~\cite{virtualkitti2,hypersim}, and the conversion from depth to range in
Section~\ref{sec:mde-to-range} assumes the same pinhole geometry. For this reason, every
captured frame is converted from a fisheye image into an equivalent pinhole image before
it is passed to the depth model.  


Equation~\ref{eq:intrinsics} defines the intrinsic matrix $K$, where $f_x$ and $f_y$ are the
focal lengths in pixels and $(c_x, c_y)$ is the principal point, the pixel where the optical
axis meets the image plane.
\begin{equation}
  K = \begin{bmatrix}
        f_x & 0   & c_x \\
        0   & f_y & c_y \\
        0   & 0   & 1
      \end{bmatrix}
  \label{eq:intrinsics}
\end{equation}
Because every ray passes through the same point and lands on a flat plane, the pinhole model
maps straight lines in the scene to straight lines in the image.




To convert fisheye frames into pinhole frames, the intrinsic matrix $K$ and the distortion
coefficients $k_1, \dots, k_4$ of the camera must first be known. These were found with the
standard checkerboard calibration method~\red{[]}. A printed checkerboard with
\red{$N_x \times N_y$} inner corners and \red{$s$}\,mm squares was photographed by the
rover's camera from \red{$M$} different positions and angles, making sure the board appeared
near the edges of the frame as well as near the center, since the edges are where the
distortion is strongest. Because the board is flat and its square size is known, the
position of every corner on the board is known exactly. The corners were then located in each
image with OpenCV's cv2.fisheye.calibrate~\cite{opencv_library}.
Figure~\ref{fig:checkerboard-calib} shows a few calibration images.
