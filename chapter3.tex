\section{Introduction to Monocular Depth Estimation}
Depth estimation is the task of recovering, for every pixel in an image,
the distance between the camera and the corresponding point in the physical scene.
Traditionally, this information has been obtained through active sensing hardware,
such as LiDAR \red{[]}, ultrasonic rangefinders\red{[]}, or time-of-flight cameras\red{[]}, or through stereo vision\red{[]},
where two calibrated cameras triangulate depth from the disparity between matched pixels. 
Depth estimation is a crucial component in many robots' navigation or mapping or \red{manipulation} 
because it allows them to perceive their environment. 


Monocular depth estimation (MDE), seeks to recover a depth map
from a single RGB image and no additional hardware. In this sense, the goal of MDE is most similar
to that of an RGB-D camera, which uses an infrared projector to cast a structured grid of laser dots
onto the scene and infers per-pixel depth \red{from how that pattern is distorted by scene geometry} \red{[]}; MDE
aims to recover the same dense, per-pixel depth map of an RGB image without
any active infrared projection. 

The MDE problem is fundamentally ill-posed: an image of a small
object placed close to the camera is, without additional context, indistinguishable from an image
of a larger object placed further away. Resolving this ambiguity requires the model to exploit
monocular depth cues, e.g., relative object size, texture gradients, occlusion boundaries,
and learned priors about typical scene geometry, rather than direct geometric triangulation \red{[??]}.
Our work leverages transformer-based machine learning models to predict 
depth of each pixel in an RGB image. MDE models are gaining more and more development
and have become more and more reliable \red{[]} in the task of predicting depth.

Monocular depth estimation is particularly attractive for embedded and resource-constrained
robotic platforms, such as the cyclops rover, because it removes the need for a
dedicated depth sensor, stereo camera rig, or LiDAR unit, all of which add cost, weight,
and power draw\red{[]}.

\section{Neural Network Architecture in Monocular Depth Estimation}
\label{sec:vit-mde}
This section gives an overview of the model used to implement the function $f$ of
Equation~\ref{eq:mde-map} on the Cyclops rover. We first discuss a key limitation of off-the-shelf
MDE models when applied to robotics: they predict relative rather than metric depth,

and give reasoning for why we chose Depth Anything V2 (DAv2)~\cite{DepthAnythingV2},
the model deployed in this work, 
chosen for its zero-shot accuracy and its lightweight variants suited to embedded hardware.
\subsection{Relative versus Metric Depth}
\label{sec:relative-depth}
Most off-the-shelf MDE models do not output meters. They output relative depth: a
dense map that is unitless, typically normalized to $[0,1]$ over the image, and correct in
ordering rather than in measurement. Relative depth describes how far the scene point at a pixel is compared with the points at
every other pixel in the image, whereas metric depth gives its absolute distance from the camera
in physical units (e.g., $50$~cm).

Relative depth is inversely related to metric depth: a higher relative depth value at a pixel
indicates that the scene point at that pixel is closer to the camera and vice versa. 
For example, a predicted relative depth value of $0.5$ asserts that the
pixel is nearer than one predicted at $0.1$; in the real scene, the pixel could be $5$ cm away or $80$ cm away. 
The raw relative depth value can not be directly used for robotics application. 
Knowing which object is nearest is of little use to a motion planner that must decide whether there is enough
clearance to pass between two of them or a gripper to accurately manipulate an object.


In this thesis, we denote
the relative depth map by $D \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$ and the model's estimate of it by
$\hat{D}$, with entries $\hat{d}(u,v)$. Equation~\eqref{eq:inverse-depth} depicts the approximate
inverse relationship between the estimated relative depth and the true metric depth at each pixel.
\begin{equation}
  \hat{d}(u,v) \;\approx\; \frac{1}{z(u,v)}
  \label{eq:inverse-depth}
\end{equation}
Relative depth mirrors stereo vision, where the disparity between two matched pixels is inversely
proportional to depth. Thus, relative depth may also be referred to as disparity
in some sources~\cite{MiDaS}.


The first reason models output relative depth is geometric, and is not a deficiency that better training could
remove. Figure~\ref{fig:scale-ambiguity} illustrates this directly: 
an object of size $s$ at distance $z$ and an object
of size $2s$ at distance $2z$ fill the same cone of light rays to the camera, and
therefore produce pixel-for-pixel identical images (figure on the right). Any MDE model will
assign both images the same output as they are identical. 
Thus why MDE is an ill-posed problem and MDE models have to output depth, it is not possible 
to get metric depth from an image alone.
Recovering metric depth requires additional information, such as a known object
size, a known camera mounting height, or a second calibrated view. Our approach uses a known camera
mounting height and calibrated points on the ground plane which we further describe in Section~\ref{sec:metric-scale}.

The second reason MDE models output relative depth is a consequence of how modern zero-shot
models are trained. Learned models, and Vision Transformers in particular, require large amounts
of training data (on the order of tens of millions of images) to be accurate~\cite{ViT,DepthAnythingV2}.
To assemble a dataset of this size, and to ensure that the model generalizes across many types
of scenes rather than overfitting to a single dataset, models such as MiDaS, DPT, and Depth
Anything are trained on a mixture of different sources. These sources include LiDAR-supervised data, RGB-D
indoor captures, stereo photographs scraped from the web, structure-from-motion reconstructions,
and frames extracted from 3D films~\cite{MiDaS,DPT}. These sources disagree about camera
intrinsics, or raw stereo data might have an camera calibration. Thus depths drawn from different datasets cannot be placed on a
common axis. The solution is to train the model to predict relative depth, which can be aligned to
the scale and shift of each source individually; we describe this alignment in
Section~\ref{sec:ssi-loss}.

We acknowledge that several recent models are trained to predict metric depth
directly. However, as Figure~\ref{fig:scale-ambiguity} illustrates, a single image does not
contain the information needed to determine absolute scale, \red{read DAV2 metric model so such models must infer metric depth from
their training data. with different camera heights and object sizes}. When
these characteristic of the scene do not match the deployment setting, for example a different camera mount height,
the predicted scale is unreliable. In our
preliminary trials, off-the-shelf metric models were noticeably less accurate on the Cyclops
rover, whose low camera mounting differs from that of typical training data, and incurred
roughly $100$~ms of additional inference latency. For these reasons, we use a relative depth model
and recover metric scale from the known geometry of the rover itself
(Section~\ref{sec:metric-scale}). A formal comparison between metric depth models and our approach
is left to future work.

\subsection{Affine-Invariant Loss Function}
\label{sec:ssi-loss}


During training, a model's parameters are adjusted to minimize a loss function, 
a measurement of the error between its prediction and the ground truth. The loss function
for an MDE model must address the mix dataset problems.

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:relative-depth}, the datasets used to train zero-shot MDE models
do not share a common scale or shift. For example, a LiDAR dataset supplies metric
depth $Z$ directly, whereas a stereo dataset supplies only disparity $D$, which can be converted to
$Z$ only with the camera's focal length and stereo baseline, quantities that are often unknown
for web-scraped stereo images and 3D films.
A conventional regression loss, such as the mean squared
error (MSE) between predicted and ground-truth depth, is therefore not suitable across these
mixed datasets. A network that correctly predicts the structure of a scene would still be penalized
because its output is not to scale with that particular dataset's ground truth.

The more suitable loss function, introduced by Ranftl et al.\ in MiDaS~\cite{MiDaS}, is
a scale-and-shift-invariant loss computed on inverse depth. The loss function rely on
a simple procedure: before the error is measured,
the prediction is aligned to the ground truth by finding the scale and shift that best fit the predicted depth
to ground truth depth, then loss is computed using ground truth and the aligned prediction.

For this alignment to be meaningful, the prediction and the ground truth must first be expressed
in the same quantity. MiDaS chooses
disparity space: rather than outputting a metric depth prediction $\hat{z}$, the network outputs a
map $\hat{d}$ that is proportional to inverse depth $1/z$ up to an unknown scale and shift. Consequently,
all ground truth is converted to disparity space.
Ground truth that is supplied as depth (for example LiDaR) is inverted to disparity $d = 1/z$ before training.
Disparity is the native output of stereo matching, so the stereo sources need no conversion.
Working in disparity space gives an addition benefit of mapping
 the sky and other effectively infinite-depth regions to a finite value near zero; and it
concentrates the precision of the representation in the near field, which is where precision
matters most for obstacle avoidance. 

After converting all groundtruth to disparity space, 
the prediction is aligned to the ground truth of
each image individually by finding an unknown scale $a$ and shift $b$.
The loss function estimates them per image before comparison,
either by fitting an that align the prediction to the ground truth via
least squares, or by normalizing the prediction and ground truth independently using robust
statistics~\cite{MiDaS}. A regression loss is then applied to the
aligned values.

\subsection{Transformers for Dense Prediction}
\red{check}
Early learned MDE models, including the original MiDaS~\cite{MiDaS}, were built on convolutional
neural network (CNN) encoder--decoder architectures, in which a convolutional backbone extracts
image features and a decoder upsamples them into a dense depth map. This design remained the
standard until the Vision Transformer (ViT)~\cite{ViT} adapted the transformer architecture,
originally developed for natural language processing~\cite{Transformer}, to images by treating an image as
a sequence of fixed-size patches. Ranftl et al.\ subsequently replaced the convolutional encoder of
MiDaS with a ViT in the Dense Prediction Transformer (DPT), improving zero-shot depth estimation
accuracy by up to $28\%$ in relative performance over a state-of-the-art fully convolutional
network~\cite{DPT}. ViT-based encoders have since become the foundation of most state-of-the-art
MDE models, including Depth Anything V2~\cite{DepthAnythingV2}.

The advantage of the ViT stems from a difference in \emph{inductive bias}, the assumptions an
architecture builds in about the structure of its input. A convolution applies a small kernel
(typically $3 \times 3$ pixels) to each neighborhood of the image, so a CNN layer sees the image
only in local chunks and assumes that nearby pixels matter most. A ViT makes no such assumption:
through self-attention, every image patch can attend to every other patch, giving the model
global context at every layer~\cite{ViT}. Global context is particularly valuable for MDE because
the cues that determine depth are rarely local. Judging how far away an obstacle is depends on
where it meets the ground plane, the perspective lines that converge toward the horizon, and its
size relative to other objects in the scene, all of which may be spread across the entire image.

A CNN can capture this context only indirectly, by enlarging its \emph{receptive field}, the region
of the input image that influences a given feature. Because each convolution is local, the
receptive field grows only as layers are stacked and the feature maps are progressively
downsampled. As noted by Ranftl et al.~\cite{DPT}, this downsampling means that feature resolution
and granularity are lost in the deeper stages of the network and are difficult to recover in the
decoder, which is a poor fit for dense prediction tasks such as MDE that require a fine-grained,
per-pixel output. A ViT, by contrast, has a global receptive field from its first layer and
maintains a constant feature resolution throughout the encoder, so global context and fine
spatial detail are available at every stage~\cite{DPT}.

\subsection{Back-Projection to 3D}

The camera is modeled as an ideal pinhole with intrinsic matrix
\begin{equation}
  K = \begin{bmatrix}
        f_x & 0   & c_x \\
        0   & f_y & c_y \\
        0   & 0   & 1
      \end{bmatrix},
  \label{eq:intrinsics}
\end{equation}
where $f_x, f_y$ are the focal lengths in pixels and $(c_x, c_y)$ is the principal point. A scene
point $(X, Y, Z)$ expressed in the camera frame projects to
\begin{equation}
  u = f_x \frac{X}{z} + c_x,
  \qquad
  v = f_y \frac{Y}{z} + c_y,
  \label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
and, given a depth $z$ at pixel $(u,v)$, the corresponding scene point is recovered by
back-projection:
\begin{equation}
  X = \frac{(u - c_x)\, z}{f_x},
  \qquad
  Y = \frac{(v - c_y)\, z}{f_y},
  \qquad
  Z = z .
  \label{eq:backprojection}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:backprojection} is the mechanism by which Section~\ref{sec:mde-to-range}
converts a depth map into the geometry of a range scan.

Equation~\eqref{eq:projection} also makes the ill-posedness described above precise. Consider
scaling the entire scene about the optical center by a factor $s > 0$, so that every scene point
$(X, Y, Z)$ is mapped to $(sX, sY, sZ)$. The projected coordinates become
\begin{equation}
  u' = f_x \frac{sX}{sz} + c_x = u,
  \qquad
  v' = f_y \frac{sY}{sz} + c_y = v,
  \label{eq:scale-ambiguity}
\end{equation}
which are unchanged, while every depth has been multiplied by $s$. The image is therefore
\emph{invariant} to a uniform rescaling of the scene, even though the depth map is not. No
function of $I$ alone, however expressive, can distinguish these cases; absolute scale is not
merely difficult to estimate from a single image, it is not present in the image at all.
