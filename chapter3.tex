\chapter{Embedded System}
\label{ch:embedded}

This chapter describes the hardware and low-level software of the Cyclops rover. It covers
the rover's components and architecture, the pulse-width modulation (PWM) that drives its
motors, its closed-loop wheel speed control, the serial link between its two boards, and
the calibration of its single camera.

\section{System Overview}
The Cyclops rover is built from five main hardware components:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{TI-RSLK rover chassis}~\cite{ti_rslk_max} -- the mobile base, including the motors,
    wheel encoders, and motor driver.
  \item \textbf{MSP432 LaunchPad}~\cite{msp432_p4} -- the microcontroller that runs the low-level
    software.
  \item \textbf{Raspberry Pi 5}~\cite{rpi5_brief} -- the onboard computer that captures camera frames
    and runs the navigation loop, the program that localizes the rover and decides how it
    moves.
  \item \textbf{MacBook Pro (M1)} -- an off-board laptop that runs monocular depth
    estimation on the frames streamed from the Raspberry Pi 5.
  \item \textbf{MKTechnic USB camera}~\cite{mktechnic_usb_camera} -- the single
    front-mounted camera, which has a $95^\circ$ field of view and is operated at
    a resolution of $640 \times 480$ pixels.
\end{itemize}

The rover runs entirely on battery power. The Raspberry Pi 5 is powered by a
Geekworm X1202 UPS HAT~\cite{geekworm_x1202}. A HAT
is an add-on board that stacks onto the Raspberry Pi's pin
header. The X1202 holds four 18650 lithium-ion cells and lets the Raspberry Pi 5 run
without a wall power supply. The TI-RSLK chassis has its own power supply: a battery
holder with six 1.2\,V rechargeable cells.

Throughout this thesis, the onboard computer is always written out as
``Raspberry Pi 5'' or ``Raspberry Pi'' and never shortened to ``Pi.'' This
avoids confusion with the proportional--integral (PI) controller that the MSP432
uses to regulate motor speed.

Figure~\ref{fig:cyclops-rover} shows the assembled Cyclops rover. The Raspberry Pi 5
sits on top of the TI-RSLK chassis, and the MSP432 LaunchPad is mounted on the chassis
below it. The USB camera is fixed to the front of the rover, facing forward and tilted
down so that the ground immediately in front of the rover is in view.

Figure~\ref{fig:rover-frame} shows the two coordinate frames used in this thesis: the
robot frame $\{R\}$ and the camera frame $\{C\}$. The origin of the robot frame, $O_R$,
is located at the bottom center of the rover, on the ground plane centered between the

${}^{R}\mathbf{p} = [{}^{R}x,\, {}^{R}y,\, {}^{R}z]^\top$ when its coordinates are
expressed in the robot frame and
${}^{C}\mathbf{p} = [{}^{C}x,\, {}^{C}y,\, {}^{C}z]^\top$ when they are expressed in the
camera frame.

The camera translation $\mathrm{cam}_t$ and pitch $\mathrm{cam}_p$ relate the robot frame
to the camera frame. The translation $\mathrm{cam}_t$ is the position of the camera origin
$O_C$ in the robot frame. The pitch $\mathrm{cam}_p$ is the angle between the camera's
optical axis and the horizontal plane of the rover, where a positive pitch tilts the camera
downward. A point measured by the camera, ${}^{C}\mathbf{p}$, is expressed in the robot frame
as ${}^{R}\mathbf{p}$ by a rigid transformation, a rotation followed by a translation that
preserves distances and angles~\cite{Forsyth_Ponce_CV}.
Equation~\ref{eq:cam-to-robot} depicts this transformation, where ${}^{R}_{C}\mathbf{R}$ is
the rotation from the camera frame to the robot frame. Chapter~\ref{ch:mde} uses this
transformation to move the points recovered from the depth map into the robot frame.
\begin{equation}
  {}^{R}\mathbf{p} = {}^{R}_{C}\mathbf{R}\;{}^{C}\mathbf{p} + \mathrm{cam}_t
  \label{eq:cam-to-robot}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:cam-rotation} depicts the rotation ${}^{R}_{C}\mathbf{R}$.
\begin{equation}
  {}^{R}_{C}\mathbf{R} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    1 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & -\sin\mathrm{cam}_p & \cos\mathrm{cam}_p \\
    0 & -\cos\mathrm{cam}_p & -\sin\mathrm{cam}_p
  \end{bmatrix}
  \label{eq:cam-rotation}
\end{equation}


\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:system-architecture}




Figure~\ref{fig:data-flow} depicts the flow of data between the laptop, the Raspberry Pi,
the MSP432, and the rover hardware. The Raspberry Pi sends camera frames to the laptop over
Wi-Fi through an SSH tunnel. The laptop runs monocular depth estimation on each frame and returns a set of
ranges, each being the distance to the nearest obstacle along one bearing, or horizontal
direction, relative to the rover's heading (Section~\ref{sec:mde-to-range}). These ranges
go to the Raspberry Pi's navigation loop, which sends target wheel speeds to the MSP432
over a universal asynchronous receiver-transmitter (UART) serial link. The MSP432 uses
these targets in its left and right PI controllers to drive the motors through an
H-bridge, a switching circuit that lets a small control signal power a motor and reverse
its direction. The left and right encoder counts return to the Raspberry Pi over the same
UART link.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \input{figures/data-flow}
  \caption{Data flow of the Cyclops rover. Text above an arrow is the data carried; text
    below it is the link it travels over. }
  \label{fig:data-flow}
\end{figure}

The computation is split across three devices:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Raspberry Pi} (high-level tasks):
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Captures camera frames and sends them to the laptop.
    \item Runs the navigation loop, which uses the returned ranges to estimate where the
      rover is in its environment and to choose its next movement
      (Section~\ref{sec:navigation}, Chapter~\ref{ch:localization}).
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{MSP432 microcontroller} (low-level tasks):
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Counts encoder pulses, which measure how far each wheel has turned
      (Section~\ref{sec:encoder}).
    \item Receives target wheel speeds from the Raspberry Pi.
    \item Runs the PI speed controllers, which keep each wheel turning at its target speed
      (Section~\ref{sec:PI}).
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Laptop}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Runs monocular depth estimation, which recovers the distance to obstacles from a
      single camera image (Chapter~\ref{ch:mde}).
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The rover is split into two boards because motor control needs precise, real-time
timing. The PI controller must update at a fixed period, and every encoder pulse must be
counted for the measured speed to be correct. The Raspberry Pi OS~\cite{rpi_os} is a
general-purpose operating system that does not guarantee when a process is
scheduled~\cite{reghenzani2019rtlinux}. Camera capture, network traffic, and other
processes can therefore delay the controller or cause pulses to be missed.

The MSP432 avoids this problem because it runs firmware, a single program executed directly
on the hardware without an operating system. Its hardware timers trigger the control update
at a fixed period, and its PWM hardware drives the motors without CPU involvement
(Section~\ref{sec:pwm}). Each encoder pulse triggers an interrupt, a hardware signal that
pauses the main program and immediately runs a short handler routine, so pulses are counted
with minimal delay~\cite{msp432_p4}.

The Raspberry Pi provides what the microcontroller lacks: a camera interface, Wi-Fi for
the SSH tunnel to the laptop, and enough memory and processing power to run navigation
and localization in a high-level language. This split isolates the timing-critical loop
from the Raspberry Pi's variable workload.

The Raspberry Pi, however, cannot run the vision transformer depth model fast enough to
support real-time localization from our preliminary tests (around 0.3 FPS). Depth inference is therefore offloaded to the
laptop, a decision discussed further in Chapter~\ref{ch:mde}.

Loop rates are reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} for reference, but system latency and power
consumption are outside the scope of this thesis.

\section{PWM}
\label{sec:pwm}

The MSP432 uses pulse-width modulation (PWM) to set the drive strength of each motor. A
PWM signal is a square wave with a fixed period that switches between high and low. The
fraction of each period that the signal spends high is called the duty cycle~\cite{valvano}.
Figure~\ref{fig:pwm-duty} compares a 10\% duty cycle, where the signal is high for only
a short part of each period, with a 90\% duty cycle, where it is high for most of the
period.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/pwm-duty}
  \caption[PWM signals at 10\% and 90\% duty cycle]{PWM signals at 10\% and 90\% duty
    cycle. Both have the same period $T_{\mathrm{PWM}}$; only the fraction of each
    period spent high changes.}
  \label{fig:pwm-duty}
\end{figure}

The MSP432 generates two PWM signals, one for each motor. Both signals come from the
hardware timer Timer\_A0. Once the timer is configured, it produces the signals without
CPU intervention. To change a duty cycle, the firmware only writes a new compare value
to the timer's register.

The timer configuration follows Valvano~\cite{valvano}. 
The timer is clocked at 1.5\,MHz, so each tick lasts about 0.667\,\textmu s. It runs in
up-down mode: it counts from 0 up to a top value $P = 7{,}500$, then back down to 0,
and repeats.
Equation~\ref{eq:pwm-period} gives the resulting PWM period
$T_{\mathrm{PWM}}$, where $f_{\mathrm{clk}}$ is the timer clock frequency.
\begin{equation}
  T_{\mathrm{PWM}} = \frac{2P}{f_{\mathrm{clk}}}
  = \frac{2 \times 7{,}500}{1.5\,\text{MHz}} = 10\,\text{ms}
  \label{eq:pwm-period}
\end{equation}
This gives a PWM frequency of
$f_{\mathrm{PWM}} = 1/T_{\mathrm{PWM}} = 1/(10\,\text{ms}) = 100\,\text{Hz}$.

Each motor's duty cycle is controlled by a single value $D$, an integer between
0 and $P$. The output goes high when the counter passes $D$ on its way down and goes low
when the counter passes $D$ on its way up. The signal is therefore high whenever the
counter is below $D$. Equation~\ref{eq:pwm-duty} gives the resulting duty cycle.
\begin{equation}
  \text{Duty cycle} = \frac{D}{P} = \frac{D}{7{,}500}
  \label{eq:pwm-duty}
\end{equation}
A value of $D = 0$ keeps the output low, so the motor is off. A value of
$D = 3{,}750$ gives a 50\% duty cycle, and values close to 7{,}500 give nearly full
power. The firmware rejects any value with $D \geq P$, so the largest usable value is
7{,}499. The value $D$ is not fixed; it is recomputed every sample period from the
output $u[k]$ of the wheel's PI speed controller, as described in Section~\ref{sec:PI}.

Each PWM signal drives the H-bridge on the TI-RSLK chassis~\cite{valvano}. A larger duty
cycle applies the battery voltage across the motor for a larger fraction of each period,
so the motor spins faster.

\section{Control Loop}
\label{sec:control-loop}

An open-loop controller sets a duty cycle and assumes it produces the desired wheel
speed. In practice, the same duty cycle does not always produce the same speed, because
the speed also depends on battery voltage, load, and differences between the two
motors~\cite{valvano}. The Cyclops rover therefore uses a closed-loop controller, which
measures each wheel's speed continuously and corrects that wheel's duty cycle
accordingly.

The controller runs in discrete time: the MSP432 measures the wheel and updates the motor
command once per sample period. A quantity written with $[k]$ is
therefore a value taken at sample $k$, and a quantity without $[k]$ stays constant
between samples. The symbols used to describe the speed loop are defined as follows.
\begin{description}
  \item[$k$] The sample index, a counter that starts at $0$ and increases by one each
    time the controller measures the wheel and updates the motor command, so
    $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ Sample $k$ occurs at time $t = k\,T_s$.
  \item[$T_s$] The sample period, the fixed time between two samples
    ($T_s = 10$\,ms).
  \item[$\dot{C}^*$] The target wheel speed sent by the Raspberry Pi, in counts per second.
  \item[$\dot{C}^*\,T_s$] The same target speed converted to counts \emph{per sample}. The encoder
    measures speed in counts for every sample period, so the target must be expressed in the
    same units before the two can be compared. Multiplying by $T_s$ performs this
    conversion; for example, a target of $\dot{C}^* = 500$ counts per second becomes
    a target speed of $\dot{C}^*\,T_s = 500 \times 0.01 = 5$ counts per sample.
  \item[{$\Delta C[k]$}] The measured wheel speed at sample $k$, in counts per sample,
    obtained from the number of encoder counts accumulated during the last sample period
    (Section~\ref{sec:encoder}).
  \item[{$e[k]$}] The speed error at sample $k$, the difference between the target
    $\dot{C}^*\,T_s$ and the measured speed $\Delta C[k]$ (Equation~\ref{eq:pi-error}).
  \item[{$u[k]$}] The controller output at sample $k$, a signed PWM duty value that sets
    the motor's drive strength and direction (Section~\ref{sec:PI}).
\end{description}

The target speed is expressed in counts per second (encoder counts per second) rather than revolutions per minute
(RPM) for two reasons. First, the target is transmitted as an integer
(Section~\ref{sec:uart-speed}). Since one RPM equals $N/60 = 6$ counts per second, an
integer in counts per second has six times finer resolution than an integer in RPM.
Second, counts are the encoder's native unit, so the MSP432 can use the target without
converting units.

Figure~\ref{fig:control-loop} shows the closed speed loop that runs on the MSP432 for
each wheel. At each sample, the measured speed $\Delta C[k]$ is subtracted from the target
$\dot{C}^*\,T_s$ to form the error $e[k]$. The PI controller converts this error into a
new duty value $u[k]$, which the PWM and H-bridge apply to the motor. The encoder
(Section~\ref{sec:encoder}) then measures the resulting speed for the next sample, and the
PI controller (Section~\ref{sec:PI}) corrects any remaining difference.

    measured speed $\Delta C[k]$ is fed back and subtracted from the target $\dot{C}^*\,T_s$;

\subsection{Encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}

An incremental rotary encoder, or simply encoder, is a sensor that converts rotation into
a train of electrical pulses, with a fixed number of pulses per
revolution~\cite{valvano}. Counting the pulses gives how far the wheel has turned, and
counting them over a known time interval gives its average rotational speed. Each wheel of
the Cyclops rover has its own encoder, which produces $N = 360$ counts per full revolution
of the wheel. The MSP432 uses the counts to measure wheel speed for the PI controller
(Section~\ref{sec:PI}) and to report to the Raspberry Pi how far each wheel has traveled.

The Cyclops rover uses quadrature encoders, which detect the direction of rotation as well
as its speed. The MSP432 keeps a signed count $C$ for each wheel, which is incremented
when the wheel moves the rover forward and decremented when it moves the rover backward.
Readers interested in how quadrature encoding works are referred to~\cite{valvano}.

Wheel speed is sampled by a periodic timer interrupt on the MSP432 that fires every
$T_s = 10$\,ms. At each sample, the interrupt handler reads the current count of each wheel
and subtracts the count it read at the previous sample. Equation~\ref{eq:encoder-speed} gives
the wheel speed $\Delta C[k]$ at sample $k$, in counts per sample period.
\begin{equation}
  \Delta C[k] = C[k] - C[k-1]
  \label{eq:encoder-speed}
\end{equation}
The left and right speeds are passed directly to the PI controller
(Section~\ref{sec:PI}), so the controller works in counts per 10\,ms rather than in
physical units.

\subsection{PI Control}
\label{sec:PI}

The controller in the speed loop is a PI controller~\cite{Alciatore_Mechatronics}. Each
wheel has its own controller, and both run inside the sampling interrupt described in
Section~\ref{sec:encoder}, immediately after the wheel speeds $\Delta C[k]$ are measured.

Equation~\ref{eq:pi-error} gives the speed error $e[k]$ at sample $k$.
\begin{equation}
  e[k] = \dot{C}^*\,T_s - \Delta C[k]
  \label{eq:pi-error}
\end{equation}
The integral term accumulates this error over time. Equation~\ref{eq:pi-integral} gives
the integral term $I[k]$, where $K_I$ is the integral gain.
\begin{equation}
  I[k] = I[k-1] + K_I\,e[k]
  \label{eq:pi-integral}
\end{equation}
The controller output is the sum of a term proportional to the current error and the
integral term. Equation~\ref{eq:pi-output} gives the output $u[k]$, where $K_P$ is the
proportional gain.
\begin{equation}
  u[k] = K_P\,e[k] + I[k]
  \label{eq:pi-output}
\end{equation}
By Equation~\ref{eq:pwm-duty}, the resulting duty cycle is $|u[k]|/7{,}500$. A positive
$u[k]$ drives the wheel forward, and a negative $u[k]$ drives it backward.
Both wheels use the same gains, $K_P = 200$ and $K_I = 20$, which were tuned empirically
on the rover. Only a small number of gain combinations were tested, because precise speed
control is not the focus of this thesis.

The performance of the controller is evaluated with a step-response test~\cite{valvano}.
Starting from rest, the target speed of a wheel is changed abruptly from zero to
$\dot{C}^* = 1{,}100$ counts per second, and the measured speed $\Delta C[k]$ is recorded
at every sample as the wheel accelerates. This target is close to the measured maximum
wheel speed of about 1{,}200 counts per second. The step response is evaluated with three
standard measures~\cite{valvano}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stability} -- whether the wheel settles to a smooth, steady speed instead
    of oscillating.
  \item \textbf{Settling time} -- the time after which the measured speed stays within
    $\pm 2\%$ of the target.
  \item \textbf{Steady-state error} -- the average of the error $e[k]$
    (Equation~\ref{eq:pi-error}) after the speed has settled.
\end{itemize}
The results of this test are presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{UART}
\label{sec:uart}

The MSP432 and the Raspberry Pi communicate over a UART link (Figure~\ref{fig:data-flow}).
UART is a serial protocol that sends data one bit at a time over a single wire in each
direction, without a shared clock. Both ends must agree in advance on the baud rate, the
number of bits sent per second, which is 115,200\,bit/s on the Cyclops rover.
Encoder counts travel from the MSP432 to the Raspberry Pi, and target wheel speeds travel
in the opposite direction.

\subsection{Encoder Count (MSP432 to Raspberry Pi)}
\label{sec:uart-slot-count}

Every 100\,ms, a timer interrupt on the MSP432 sends the current left and right encoder
counts (Section~\ref{sec:encoder}) to the Raspberry Pi. The firmware briefly disables
interrupts while it copies the two counts, so both values come from the same instant.
Each count is a signed 32-bit integer and is sent in big-endian order, with the most
significant byte first.
Table~\ref{tab:slot-count-packet} shows the resulting 10-byte packet.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Encoder count packet sent from the MSP432 to the Raspberry Pi.}
  \label{tab:slot-count-packet}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \hline
    Byte & Field & Description \\
    \hline
    0     & Start    & Constant \texttt{0xAA} \\
    1--4  & $C_L$    & Left encoder count \\
    5--8  & $C_R$    & Right encoder count \\
    9     & Checksum & XOR of bytes 1--8 \\
    \hline
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The start byte marks the beginning of a packet so that the Raspberry Pi can find
the packet boundaries in the byte stream. Equation~\ref{eq:uart-checksum} defines the
checksum $X$, where $b_1, \dots, b_8$ are the eight data bytes and $\oplus$ denotes
bitwise XOR.
\begin{equation}
  X = b_1 \oplus b_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus b_8
  \label{eq:uart-checksum}
\end{equation}
The Raspberry Pi recomputes $X$ from the received data bytes and discards the packet if
the result does not match the received checksum. This check detects packets corrupted
during transmission and prevents corrupted encoder counts from reaching the localization
pipeline.

\subsection{Target Wheel Speed (Raspberry Pi to MSP432)}
\label{sec:uart-speed}

The Raspberry Pi sends the target left and right wheel speeds to the MSP432, where they
become the setpoints $\dot{C}^*_L$ and $\dot{C}^*_R$ of the PI controllers
(Section~\ref{sec:PI}). Unlike the encoder count packet, the speed command is not sent on a
fixed schedule. The Raspberry Pi sends a new command only when the navigation loop
(Section~\ref{sec:navigation}) finishes an iteration, so the time between commands depends
on how long each iteration takes.

Each target speed is given in counts per second (Section~\ref{sec:control-loop}) and sent as
a signed 16-bit integer in big-endian order. The sign gives the direction of rotation, with
positive values driving the wheel forward. Table~\ref{tab:speed-packet} shows the resulting
6-byte packet.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Speed command packet sent from the Raspberry Pi to the MSP432.}
  \label{tab:speed-packet}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \hline
    Byte & Field & Description \\
    \hline
    0     & Start    & Constant \texttt{0xAA} \\
    1--2  & $\dot{C}^*_L$ & Left target speed\\
    3--4  & $\dot{C}^*_R$ & Right target speed\\
    5     & Checksum & XOR of bytes 1--4 \\
    \hline
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The packet uses the same framing as the encoder count packet: a \texttt{0xAA} start byte
followed by an XOR checksum. Equation~\ref{eq:uart-speed-checksum} applies the checksum of
Equation~\ref{eq:uart-checksum} to the four data bytes $b_1, \dots, b_4$ of the speed
packet.
\begin{equation}
  X = b_1 \oplus b_2 \oplus b_3 \oplus b_4
  \label{eq:uart-speed-checksum}
\end{equation}
Like the Raspberry Pi, the MSP432 recomputes $X$ from the received data bytes and discards the packet if the
result does not match the received checksum. When a packet is discarded, both setpoints are
set to zero, stopping the rover until the next valid command arrives.

\section{Camera}
\label{sec:camera}

The MKTechnic USB camera uses a wide-angle fisheye lens. The wide lens lets the rover see
more of the scene in front of it, but it also distorts straight lines in the image, most
visibly near the edges of the frame. This is a problem for two reasons. First,
the monocular depth estimation models used in this thesis (Chapter~\ref{ch:mde}) are
trained mostly on images taken with ordinary pinhole cameras~\cite{virtualkitti2,hypersim}.
Second, the conversion from depth to range in Section~\ref{sec:mde-to-range} assumes
pinhole geometry. Every captured frame is therefore converted into an equivalent pinhole
image before it is passed to the depth model.

In the pinhole model, every light ray from the scene passes through a single point, the
optical center, and lands on a flat image plane. As a result, straight lines in the scene
remain straight in the image~\cite{Forsyth_Ponce_CV}. The intrinsic matrix $K$ describes
how a pinhole camera maps a point in the camera frame to a pixel.
Equation~\ref{eq:intrinsics} defines $K$, where $f_x$ and $f_y$ are the focal lengths in
pixels and $(c_x, c_y)$ is the principal point, the pixel where the optical axis meets the
image plane.
\begin{equation}
  K = \begin{bmatrix}
        f_x & 0   & c_x \\
        0   & f_y & c_y \\
        0   & 0   & 1
      \end{bmatrix}
  \label{eq:intrinsics}
\end{equation}

To convert fisheye frames into pinhole frames, the intrinsic matrix $K$ and the distortion
coefficients $k_1, \dots, k_4$ of the camera must first be known. The distortion
coefficients belong to OpenCV's fisheye camera model and describe how strongly the lens
bends each incoming ray as a function of its angle from the optical
axis~\cite{opencv_library}. Both $K$ and $k_1, \dots, k_4$ were found with the standard
checkerboard calibration method~\cite{zhang2000flexible}. A printed checkerboard with
$6 \times 8$ inner corners and 23\,mm squares was photographed by the rover's camera from
18 different positions and angles. The board was placed near the edges of the frame as well
as near the center, because distortion is strongest at the edges. Since the board is flat
and its square size is known, the position of every corner on the board is known exactly.
The corners were located in each image with OpenCV's
\texttt{cv2.\allowbreak findChessboardCorners} function. Then $K$ and $k_1, \dots, k_4$ were
then estimated from these corners with \texttt{cv2.\allowbreak fisheye.\allowbreak
calibrate}~\cite{opencv_library}. Figure~\ref{fig:checkerboard-calib} shows a few
calibration images, and Table~\ref{tab:camera-intrinsics} lists the resulting parameters.

cropped. The pinhole image is then passed to the monocular depth estimation model
