\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:lit-review}

This chapter reviews the prior work that the Cyclops rover builds on and places this thesis
within it. Section~\ref{sec:rw-range} reviews the sensors that mobile robots conventionally use to
measure distance, and why they are hard to justify on a small, battery-powered platform.
Section~\ref{sec:rw-mde} traces the development of monocular depth estimation from hand-crafted
cues to the transformer-based models used in this work. Section~\ref{sec:rw-loc} reviews
localization with range sensors. Section~\ref{sec:rw-mde-nav} reviews work that uses monocular
depth in place of a range sensor for robot navigation, including the work most closely related to
this thesis, and Section~\ref{sec:rw-summary} summarizes how the Cyclops rover differs from it.

\section{Range Sensing on Small Mobile Robots}
\label{sec:rw-range}

Mobile robots have traditionally measured the distance to their surroundings with a dedicated
range sensor. Early indoor robots used rings of ultrasonic sonar sensors, which are inexpensive but
have wide beams, so a single reading only places an obstacle somewhere within a
cone~\cite{Moravec_Sonar}. Planar LiDAR sweeps a laser across the scene and returns accurate ranges
at a fine angular resolution~\cite{kawata2005urg}, and it became the standard sensor for indoor
localization and mapping~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic, hess2016cartographer}. 
Dense depth images can be obtained from time-of-flight cameras~\cite{ToF}, from structured-light
RGB-D cameras such as the Microsoft Kinect~\cite{rgbd}, or from a pair of calibrated cameras that
triangulate depth from the disparity between matched pixels (stereo cameras)~\cite{stereo}.

Each of these sensors adds cost, weight, and power draw, and each is a second sensor alongside the
camera that most robots already carry for scene understanding. On a small, battery-powered
platform such as the Cyclops rover, that overhead is hard to justify. This motivates the use of a
single camera for both scene understanding and range sensing, which requires recovering depth from
a single image.

\section{Monocular Depth Estimation}
\label{sec:rw-mde}

Monocular depth estimation (MDE) recovers a dense depth map from a single RGB image. Early work
used Markov random fields over image superpixels~\cite{Saxena2009Make3D}, simple geometric
assumptions about the layout of the scene~\cite{Hoiem2005PopUp}, or non-parametric methods that
transfer depth from similar images in a database~\cite{Karsch2014DepthTransfer}. % \red{[]}s, in order: Saxena et al., Make3D (TPAMI 2009); Hoiem et al., Automatic Photo Pop-up (SIGGRAPH 2005); Karsch et al., Depth Transfer (TPAMI 2014). All three are cited in the related work of the MiDaS paper.
Eigen et al.~\cite{Eigen} showed that a deep convolutional network could instead be trained end to
end to predict a depth map directly from the image, supervised by ground-truth depth from RGB-D
and LiDAR datasets such as NYU Depth v2~\cite{NYUD} and KITTI~\cite{kitti}. % \red{[]}: Geiger et al., "Are we ready for autonomous driving? The KITTI vision benchmark suite" (CVPR 2012)
Convolutional encoder--decoder networks dominated the field in the years that
followed~\cite{MiDaS}. % \red{[]}: an MDE survey, e.g. Ming et al., "Deep learning for monocular depth estimation: A review" (Neurocomputing 2021)
Ranftl et al.~\cite{DPT} later replaced the convolutional encoder with a vision transformer
(ViT)~\cite{ViT}, the resulting model, DPT, produced
finer-grained and more globally coherent depth maps than fully convolutional
networks~\cite{DPT}.

Beyond network architecture, MDE models also differ in what kind of depth they output: metric
depth in meters, or relative depth that is correct only up to an unknown scale and shift.
Predicting relative depth allows a model to learn from many datasets at once, even when their
depth labels do not share a common scale. Ranftl et al.~\cite{MiDaS} used this to train MiDaS on
a mixture of heterogeneous datasets, with a loss that ignores the unknown scale and shift of each
image's depth. The model gave up metric output in exchange for zero-shot transfer to scenes it had
never seen. Depth Anything V2~\cite{DepthAnythingV2}, the model used in this thesis, keeps this
relative formulation with a DINOv2~\cite{DINOv2} pretrained ViT encoder and a DPT decoder, and is
trained first on synthetic images and then on a large set of pseudo-labeled real images. Other
models have tried to recover metric depth without giving up generalization, either by adding
metric heads fine-tuned on in-domain data~\cite{zoedepth} or by learning metric scale together with
scene geometry from very large training sets~\cite{MoGe}. Chapter~\ref{ch:mde} explains why the
Cyclops rover uses a relative model instead, and recovers metric scale from its own geometry.

\section{Localization with Range Sensors}
\label{sec:rw-loc}

Localization and mapping are two related but distinct problems. Localization asks where the robot
is, given a map of the environment that is already known; mapping asks what the environment looks
like, given that the robot's poses are already known~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic}. Simultaneous
localization and mapping (SLAM) removes both assumptions and estimates the map and the robot's pose
together, as the robot explores an environment it has never seen~\cite{durrantwhyte2006slam}. % \red{[]}: e.g. Durrant-Whyte and Bailey, "Simultaneous localization and mapping: Part I" (IEEE RAM 2006)
This thesis addresses localization only: the map of the environment is assumed to be given.

Localization estimates a robot's pose within a map from how it has moved and what its sensors
observe~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic}. Monte Carlo localization (MCL), introduced by Dellaert et
al.~\cite{dellaert1999monte}, represents the robot's belief about its pose with a set of weighted samples, or
particles, and updates them with a particle filter. % \red{[]}: Dellaert, Fox, Burgard, Thrun, "Monte Carlo Localization for Mobile Robots" (ICRA 1999)
Because a particle set can represent a belief with several peaks, MCL can localize a robot whose
starting pose is unknown, which a Kalman filter tracking a single Gaussian
cannot~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic}. MCL is usually paired with sonar or LiDAR: the measured ranges
are compared against the ranges that would be seen from each particle's pose in the map, using a
beam or likelihood-field measurement model~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic}. Its adaptive variant,
AMCL~\cite{Fox2003KLD}, is the default localization method in the ROS~2 navigation stack~\cite{Macenski2020Marathon2} and is
widely used on LiDAR-equipped indoor robots. % \red{[]}s, in order: Fox, "KLD-Sampling: Adaptive Particle Filters" (NeurIPS 2001); Macenski et al., "The Marathon 2: A Navigation System" (IROS 2020)

Cameras have also been used for localization, by matching image features or appearance against a
visual map of the environment~\cite{dellaert1999condensation}. % \red{[]}: e.g. Dellaert, Burgard, Fox, Thrun, "Using the CONDENSATION algorithm for robust, vision-based mobile robot localization" (CVPR 1999)
These approaches need a map of how the environment looks, rather than of its geometry. Monocular
depth estimation offers a third option: a camera can produce a range scan that is used by the
standard range-based measurement model without modification, so the map only has to describe the
geometry of the environment. Chapter~\ref{ch:localization} takes this approach.

\section{Monocular Depth for Robot Navigation}
\label{sec:rw-mde-nav}

Once a depth map has been predicted, it can be back-projected into a 3D point cloud, so that
algorithms written for LiDAR can consume camera data unchanged. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2019pseudo} called this
representation \emph{pseudo-LiDAR}, and showed that it substantially narrowed the gap between
camera-only and LiDAR-based 3D object detection for autonomous driving. % \red{[]}: Wang et al., "Pseudo-LiDAR from Visual Depth Estimation: Bridging the Gap in 3D Object Detection for Autonomous Driving" (CVPR 2019)
Section~\ref{sec:mde-to-range} applies the same idea to a small ground robot, reducing the point
cloud to a planar range scan. Other work skips the explicit depth estimate and trains a network to
map camera images directly to steering commands~\cite{bojarski2016end}. This avoids calibrating depth, but it
does not give the robot a metric model of its surroundings that a planner or localizer can
use. % \red{[]}: e.g. Bojarski et al., "End to End Learning for Self-Driving Cars" (arXiv 2016); the CNN maze-navigation paper in ../paper/ may also fit here

The work most closely related to this thesis is that of Louw et al.~\cite{practical}, who evaluated Depth Anything V2 [30] as a replacement for LiDAR in the navigation of a differential-drive robot.
They ran the largest (335.3M-parameter) indoor metric variant of DAv2 without fine-tuning at 12 Hz on an Intel i9 CPU with an NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU.
Because the predicted depth still showed a consistent scale offset, they multiplied it by a constant factor tuned by hand to align the MDE point cloud with obstacle positions measured by the robot’s LiDAR.
The resulting point cloud fed a voxel-layer costmap in the ROS 2 navigation stack (Nav2), which used it for local obstacle avoidance, while the robot’s pose came from wheel odometry alone.
The robot completed three obstacle courses without collision in every trial, with travel distances and replanning comparable to a LiDAR baseline.
However, MDE placed distant obstacles too close (about 2.2 m for an obstacle at 3 m), so only obstacles within 2 m were used for planning.

This thesis differs from that work in three ways.
First, in what the depth is used for: Louw et al. used MDE only to detect obstacles near the robot and did not use it for mapping or localization.
On the Cyclops rover, the depth-derived range scan is the measurement of Monte Carlo localization in a known map (Chapter 5), so the camera, rather than a LiDAR, corrects the drift of wheel odometry.
Second, in how metric scale is recovered: rather than applying one scale factor tuned against a LiDAR, the Cyclops rover uses the relative model and re-estimates both the scale and the shift of 
its disparity on every frame from ground points directly in front of it (Section 4.3.3).
No second range sensor is needed at any stage.
Louw et al. themselves proposed ground-plane-based depth scaling as future work.
Third, in platform: the Cyclops rover runs DAv2-Small, about 13 times smaller, on a low-cost battery-powered robot whose only sensor besides the wheel encoders is the camera (Chapter 3).

\label{sec:rw-summary}

The Cyclops rover and the robot of Louw et al.~\cite{practical} both replace a LiDAR with a single
camera running Depth Anything V2, but they ask different things of it. Louw et al. used a large
metric model on a desktop-class GPU to detect nearby obstacles, with a scale factor tuned once
against a LiDAR and a pose that came from wheel odometry alone. The Cyclops rover uses a small
relative model on low-cost hardware, recovers metric scale on every frame from the ground in front
of it, and uses the resulting range scan in the role that a LiDAR scan plays for AMCL: as the
measurement that corrects odometry drift in Monte Carlo localization. This closes the loop between
monocular depth and localization, which Louw et al. left open, without a second range sensor at any
stage. Chapter~\ref{ch:mde} describes the depth pipeline, and Chapter~\ref{ch:localization} the
localization built on it.
