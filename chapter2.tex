\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:lit-review}

This chapter reviews the prior work that the Cyclops rover builds on and places this thesis
within it. Section~\ref{sec:rw-range} reviews the sensors that mobile robots conventionally use to
measure distance, and why they are hard to justify on a small, battery-powered platform.
Section~\ref{sec:rw-mde} traces the development of monocular depth estimation from hand-crafted
cues to the transformer-based models used in this work. Section~\ref{sec:rw-loc} reviews
localization with range sensors. Section~\ref{sec:rw-mde-nav} reviews work that uses monocular
depth in place of a range sensor for robot navigation, including the work most closely related to
this thesis, and Section~\ref{sec:rw-summary} summarizes how the Cyclops rover differs from it.

\section{Range Sensing on Small Mobile Robots}
\label{sec:rw-range}

Mobile robots have traditionally measured the distance to their surroundings with a dedicated
range sensor. Early indoor robots used rings of ultrasonic sonar sensors, which are inexpensive but
have wide beams, so a single reading only places an obstacle somewhere within a
cone~\cite{Moravec_Sonar}. Planar LiDAR sweeps a laser across the scene and returns accurate ranges
at a fine angular resolution~\cite{kawata2005urg}, and it became the standard sensor for indoor
localization and mapping~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic, hess2016cartographer}. 
Dense depth images can be obtained from time-of-flight cameras~\cite{ToF}, from structured-light
RGB-D cameras such as the Microsoft Kinect~\cite{rgbd}, or from a pair of calibrated cameras that
triangulate depth from the disparity between matched pixels (stereo cameras)~\cite{stereo}.

Each of these sensors adds cost, weight, and power draw, and each is a second sensor alongside the
camera that most robots already carry for scene understanding. On a small, battery-powered
platform such as the Cyclops rover, that overhead is hard to justify. This motivates the use of a
single camera for both scene understanding and range sensing, which requires recovering depth from
a single image.

\section{Monocular Depth Estimation}
\label{sec:rw-mde}

Monocular depth estimation (MDE) recovers a dense depth map, a distance for every pixel, from a
single RGB image. Early work relied on hand-designed rules: statistical models of how depth varies
between neighboring image regions~\cite{Saxena2009Make3D}, simple assumptions about the layout of
the scene, such as a flat ground and vertical walls~\cite{Hoiem2005PopUp}, or copying depth from
similar-looking images in a database~\cite{Karsch2014DepthTransfer}.
Eigen et al.~\cite{Eigen} showed that a deep convolutional neural network could instead learn to
predict depth directly from the image. The network was trained on images paired with depth
measured by a real sensor, from datasets such as NYU Depth v2~\cite{NYUD}, recorded with an RGB-D
camera, and KITTI~\cite{kitti}, recorded with a LiDAR.
In the years that followed, most MDE networks used an encoder--decoder design: an encoder
compresses the image into a compact set of features, and a decoder expands those features back
into a full-resolution depth map~\cite{MiDaS,Ming2021MDEReview}.
Ranftl et al.~\cite{DPT} later replaced the convolutional encoder with a vision transformer
(ViT)~\cite{ViT}. Each layer of a convolutional encoder looks at only a small neighborhood of
pixels, whereas each layer of a ViT lets every part of the image exchange information with every
other part. The
resulting model, DPT, produced sharper depth maps that were more consistent across the whole
scene.

Beyond network architecture, MDE models also differ in what kind of depth they output: metric
depth in meters, or relative depth that is correct only up to an unknown scale and shift.
Predicting relative depth allows a model to learn from many datasets at once, even when their
depth labels do not share a common scale. Ranftl et al.~\cite{MiDaS} used this to train MiDaS on
a mixture of heterogeneous datasets, with a loss that ignores the unknown scale and shift of each
image's depth. The model gave up metric output in exchange for zero-shot transfer to scenes it had
never seen. Depth Anything V2 (DAv2)~\cite{DepthAnythingV2}, the model used in this thesis, keeps this
relative formulation with a DINOv2~\cite{DINOv2} pretrained ViT encoder and a DPT decoder, and is
trained first on synthetic images and then on a large set of pseudo-labeled real images. Other
models have tried to recover metric depth without giving up generalization, either by adding
metric heads fine-tuned on in-domain data~\cite{zoedepth} or by learning metric scale together with
scene geometry from very large training sets~\cite{MoGe}. Chapter~\ref{ch:mde} explains why the
Cyclops rover uses a relative model instead, and recovers metric scale from its own geometry.

\section{Localization with Range Sensors}
\label{sec:rw-loc}

Localization and mapping are two related but distinct problems. Localization asks where the robot
is, given a map of the environment that is already known; mapping asks what the environment looks
like, given that the robot's poses (its position and heading over time) are already
known~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic}. Simultaneous
localization and mapping (SLAM) removes both assumptions and estimates the map and the robot's pose
together, as the robot explores an environment it has never seen~\cite{durrantwhyte2006slam}.
This thesis addresses localization only: the map of the environment is assumed to be given.

Monte Carlo localization (MCL), introduced by Dellaert et al.~\cite{dellaert1999monte}, keeps a
large set of guesses, called particles, of where the robot might be. As the robot moves, each
particle is moved the same way, and when the sensors take a measurement, particles whose pose
agrees with it are kept while the rest are discarded.
Because the particles can be spread across several places at once, MCL can keep track of more than
one candidate location, for example two identical-looking hallways, until later measurements rule
all but one out. This lets MCL localize a robot whose starting pose is unknown. A Kalman filter,
the other common approach, cannot do this, because it tracks only a single best estimate and its
uncertainty~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic}.
MCL is usually paired with sonar or LiDAR. Each particle is scored by comparing the measured ranges
with the ranges the robot would see if it were at that particle's pose in the
map~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic}; Section~\ref{sec:measurement-model} describes this measurement
model in detail. Adaptive MCL (AMCL)~\cite{Fox2003KLD} adds more particles when the robot is
uncertain and removes them when it is confident. AMCL is the default localization method in the
navigation stack of ROS~2~\cite{Macenski2020Marathon2}, a widely used open-source robotics software
framework, and is common on LiDAR-equipped indoor robots.

Cameras have also been used for localization, by matching image features or appearance against a
visual map of the environment~\cite{dellaert1999condensation}.
These approaches need a map of how the environment looks, rather than of its geometry. Monocular
depth estimation offers a third option: a camera can produce a range scan that is used by the
standard range-based measurement model without modification. Chapter~\ref{ch:localization} takes this approach.

\section{Monocular Depth for Robot Navigation}
\label{sec:rw-mde-nav}

Once a depth map has been predicted, each pixel can be back-projected along its camera ray to the
predicted distance, turning the image into a set of 3D points (a point cloud). Since a LiDAR
produces the same kind of point cloud, algorithms written for LiDAR can then use camera data unchanged. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2019pseudo} called this
representation \emph{pseudo-LiDAR}, and showed that it substantially narrowed the gap between
camera-only and LiDAR-based 3D object detection for autonomous driving.
Section~\ref{sec:mde-to-range} applies the same idea to a small ground robot, reducing the point
cloud to a planar range scan. Other work skips the explicit depth estimate and trains a network to
map camera images directly to steering commands~\cite{bojarski2016end}. This avoids calibrating depth, but it
does not give the robot a metric model of its surroundings that a planner or localizer can
use.

The work most closely related to this thesis is that of Louw et al.~\cite{practical}, who evaluated DAv2 as a replacement for LiDAR in the navigation of a wheeled indoor robot.
They used the largest DAv2 model, with 335.3M parameters, in its version trained to output metric depth for indoor scenes, without any further training.
It ran at 12~Hz on a desktop-class computer with a dedicated GPU.
The predicted depth was consistently off by a scale factor, so they multiplied it by a constant tuned by hand until the predicted obstacle positions matched those measured by the robot's LiDAR.
The resulting point cloud was added to a costmap, a grid that marks which cells around the robot are blocked, which the ROS~2 navigation stack (Nav2) used to steer around obstacles.
The robot's pose came from wheel odometry alone, that is, from counting wheel rotations, with no correction from any sensor.
The robot completed three obstacle courses without a collision in every trial, and its travel distances were comparable to those of a LiDAR baseline.
However, the predicted depth placed distant obstacles too close (about 2.2~m for an obstacle at 3~m), so only obstacles within 2~m were used for planning.

This thesis differs from that work in three ways.
The first difference is what the depth is used for.
Louw et al. used MDE only to detect obstacles near the robot, not for mapping or localization.
On the Cyclops rover, the range scan derived from depth is the measurement used by Monte Carlo localization in a known map (Chapter~\ref{ch:localization}).
The camera, rather than a LiDAR, therefore corrects the error that builds up in wheel odometry over time.
The second difference is the hardware.
The Cyclops rover uses DAv2-Small, which has about 25M parameters, roughly one-thirteenth as many as the model used by Louw et al.
The model runs off the robot, on a laptop (a MacBook Pro with an Apple M1 chip) using its integrated GPU through Metal Performance Shaders (MPS), at about 4~Hz, compared with 12~Hz on the desktop GPU used by Louw et al.
The robot itself is low-cost and battery-powered, and its only sensors are the camera and the wheel encoders (Chapter~\ref{ch:embedded}).
The third and most important difference is how metric scale is recovered.
The scale and shift determine how every predicted value is converted into a distance in meters, so any error in them carries into every range the robot measures.
Instead of one scale factor tuned against a LiDAR, the Cyclops rover uses the relative model and re-estimates both the scale and the shift on every frame.
It does so from points on the ground directly in front of the robot, whose distance is known from the height and tilt of the camera (Section~\ref{sec:metric-scale}).
Louw et al. themselves proposed scaling depth from the ground plane as future work.
Using the ground plane and the known height of the camera to recover metric scale is an established technique in monocular visual odometry~\cite{Kitt2011PlanarRoad}.
This thesis applies the same idea to a relative depth model, where both a scale and a shift must be recovered rather than a scale alone.
