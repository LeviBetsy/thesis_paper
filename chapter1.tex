A mobile robot that moves through a building has to know where it is. It needs this to plan a
path to a goal, to avoid the walls and objects around it, and to know when it has arrived. Wheel
encoders tell the robot how far it has moved, but their small errors add up as it drives, so the
robot must also observe its surroundings and compare what it sees with a map. This process is
called localization~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic}.

Most robots that localize this way measure the distance to their surroundings with a dedicated
range sensor, such as a planar LiDAR or a ring of ultrasonic sensors
(Chapter~\ref{ch:lit-review}). These sensors work well, but each one adds cost, weight, and power
draw, and each is a second sensor alongside the camera that most robots already carry. On a
small, battery-powered robot, that overhead is hard to justify.

A camera is cheap, light, and draws little power, but a single image does not directly say how
far away anything is. Monocular depth estimation (MDE) addresses this by predicting a depth value
for every pixel of a single image with a neural network. Recent MDE models built on the vision
transformer (ViT)~\cite{ViT}, such as Depth Anything V2~\cite{DepthAnythingV2}, produce detailed
depth maps even for scenes they were never trained on. If such a model is accurate enough, one
camera could serve as both the robot's eyes and its range sensor.

This thesis asks whether that is the case: \emph{is monocular depth estimation good enough to
replace a dedicated range sensor for robot localization?} To answer it, we built the Cyclops
rover, a small wheeled robot whose only view of its surroundings is a single front-mounted
camera, and used the depth predicted from that camera to localize the rover in a known map.

\section{Background}

The Cyclops rover is built on the TI-RSLK chassis~\cite{ti_rslk_max} and carries a single
front-mounted USB camera, a Raspberry Pi~5, and an MSP432 microcontroller
(Chapter~\ref{ch:embedded}). Its task is to drive to a goal picked on a map of its environment,
which it does by repeating the same loop. The Raspberry Pi captures a camera frame and sends it to
an off-board laptop, which runs Depth Anything V2 to predict a depth map. The predicted depth is
calibrated to meters using points on the floor directly in front of the rover, and is then reduced
to a range scan: a list of distances to the nearest obstacle in each direction, the same form of
measurement that a planar LiDAR produces (Chapter~\ref{ch:mde}). Back on the Raspberry Pi, Monte
Carlo localization (MCL) combines this range scan with the wheel encoder counts to estimate the
rover's position and heading in the map, and a path planner uses that estimate to send target
wheel speeds to the MSP432 (Chapter~\ref{ch:localization}). The MSP432 then holds each wheel at its
target speed with a closed-loop controller.

Because the range scan has the same form as a LiDAR scan, the localization itself follows standard
methods~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic}. What changes is the sensor: the measurement model of MCL now
has to work with ranges predicted by a neural network instead of ranges measured by a laser.

\section{Research Question}
\label{sec:research-question}

The central question of this thesis is whether monocular depth estimation is good enough to serve
as the range sensor for robot localization. The answer is not obvious, because a range scan
predicted from a single image differs from a LiDAR scan in several ways.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Unknown scale.} A single image cannot distinguish a small object nearby from a
  large object far away, so most MDE models predict only relative depth, which is correct in
  ordering but not in meters (Section~\ref{sec:relative-depth}). Localization needs ranges in
  meters, so the scale must be recovered from somewhere else.

  \item \textbf{Larger error.} A LiDAR measures each range directly, with an error of a few
  centimeters or less~\red{[]}. An MDE range is a prediction, and its error is much larger and
  depends on the scene.

  \item \textbf{Correlated error.} Every ray of an MDE range scan is cut from the same depth map,
  predicted by the same network in a single pass, so one mistake, such as a poorly estimated
  scale, can shift many rays at once.

  \item \textbf{Narrow field of view and short range.} A planar LiDAR typically sees all the way
  around the robot and out to several meters~\red{[]}. A single forward-facing camera sees only
  what is in front of it, and MDE becomes unreliable at longer distances, so the Cyclops rover
  uses ranges only up to $0.40$~m.

  \item \textbf{Low update rate.} Predicting depth takes far longer than a LiDAR sweep. On the
  Cyclops rover, a new range scan arrives about three times per second, and only after the camera
  frame has made a round trip to the laptop.
\end{itemize}

Each of these differences makes the measurement less informative, and localization relies on the
measurement to correct the drift of wheel odometry. This thesis tests whether, despite these
differences, an MDE range scan carries enough information for the rover to localize itself and
navigate. The question is broken into three parts:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Can the relative depth predicted by Depth Anything V2 be converted into metric depth on a
  small rover, without any additional sensor?
  \item Can that metric depth be turned into a range scan that a standard range-based measurement
  model can use without modification?
  \item Does Monte Carlo localization with this range scan estimate the rover's pose accurately
  enough for the rover to drive to a goal?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A method that converts the relative disparity of Depth Anything V2 into metric depth on
  every frame, by fitting its unknown scale and shift to points on the floor whose depth is known
  from the camera's fixed mounting (Section~\ref{sec:metric-scale}).

  \item A pipeline that turns the metric depth map into a planar range scan, by back-projecting it
  into a 3D point cloud, removing the floor, and keeping the nearest obstacle in each direction
  (Section~\ref{sec:mde-to-range}).

  \item A Monte Carlo localization system that uses this MDE range scan as its measurement, with a
  beam measurement model whose ray casting matches the geometry of the camera
  (Chapter~\ref{ch:localization}).

  \item The Cyclops rover itself: a low-cost, battery-powered robot that combines these parts with
  wheel odometry and path planning to drive to a goal using a single camera
  (Chapters~\ref{ch:embedded} and~\ref{ch:localization}).

  \item An evaluation of the full system on the rover, which compares the path estimated by MCL
  against the true path recorded by an overhead camera and measures how often the rover reaches
  its goal (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:outline}

The rest of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{ch:lit-review} reviews prior work on
range sensing, monocular depth estimation, and localization. Chapter~\ref{ch:embedded} describes
the hardware and low-level software of the Cyclops rover. Chapter~\ref{ch:mde} explains how depth
is predicted from a single image, calibrated to meters, and turned into a range scan.
Chapter~\ref{ch:localization} describes the Monte Carlo localization and path planning built on
that range scan. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} presents the experimental results,
Chapter~\ref{ch:future-work} lists directions for future work, and Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}
answers the research question.
